# Prior surveys of legislative engagement with AI personhood

**Date:** 2026-08-10 · Desk research, web sources. Verification status noted per item.

## Verdict

The paper is **not** the first analysis of legislation on AI personhood, and the US state
bills are **not** the first legislative engagement with AI legal status. Both claims have
counter-examples. But nobody is running a **legislation-focused registry** of the kind we
proposed, so the project's niche holds.

**Correction (2026-08-10).** I first called Jaynes (2024) "the significant miss" and listed
it as a prior analysis. Having now read the full text: it appears in **AI & Society's
"Curmudgeon Corner"**, an editorially-reviewed opinion column, not a peer-reviewed research
section. It is a short commentary of a few pages, not a systematic survey.

That materially weakens my earlier claim. The paper says no *systematic* analysis exists and
that the bills drew "scattered media coverage and a few critical op-eds" — Jaynes fits that
description, so **the paper's novelty claim survives**. What remains is a narrower point:
the paper cites Liebman as "one recent analysis of the Exclusion Bills in Utah and Idaho"
while not citing Jaynes, who did the same thing two years earlier in a Springer journal.
That is a citation gap worth mentioning to Austin, not a refutation.

Kept in Literature rather than Evidence (journal-hosted, DOI, has a reference list), typed
as `commentary`. Mass-media op-eds — Berg & Rosenblatt in the WSJ, Alexander & Simon in the
Ohio Capital Journal — stay in Evidence. The line: journal-hosted and citable is literature;
newspaper opinion is evidence.

**Unassessed, lower confidence** — surfaced but not evaluated for quality or peer review:

## B. The EU 2017 "electronic personhood" episode

A prior instance of legislative engagement with AI legal status, with its own literature:

- **February 2017:** the European Parliament adopted a resolution proposing legal
  personality for sophisticated autonomous robots — "electronic persons" — as a possible
  remedy for civil liability. Preceded by a Legal Affairs Committee report, January 2017.
- **April 2018:** an open letter from 150+ experts in AI, robotics, law and ethics across
  14 countries opposed it, arguing electronic personhood was "ideological and nonsensical",
  based on a view of robots "distorted by science fiction", and potentially in breach of
  human rights law.
- **Secondary literature** includes *Electronic personhood for artificial intelligence in
  the workplace* (Computer Law & Security Review), Maastricht's *The timeline of
  e-personhood*, and a JCOM conference panel on "e-persons".

**This undercuts the paper's framing.** The abstract calls the Exclusion Bills "the
first-ever meaningful legislative engagement with AI legal status." The EP resolution was
legislative engagement with AI legal status, it was arguably meaningful — it triggered an
international expert mobilisation — and it predates Idaho HB 720 by five years.

The claim is defensible if narrowed: first *enacted, binding* law; or first legislation
*denying* personhood (the EP proposal sought to grant it); or first at US *state* level.
As written it is vulnerable, and it is the kind of superlative that invites an easy
rebuttal from anyone who remembers 2017.

**For the tracker:** state the count and scope — "23 bills across 12 states since 2022" —
and let readers judge. No "first", no "largest". Same discipline we apply elsewhere.

## C. Existing trackers

**Directly adjacent — and a potential collaborator, not a competitor:**

**AI Rights and Legal Personhood Tracker**, `naturalandartificiallaw.com`, maintained by
**Matthew Lee**, a barrister in England & Wales. Free. Covers AI personhood, inventorship,
authorship and standing across the UK, ECtHR, EU, US, Australia, Canada, New Zealand and
international bodies. Table format, last updated 10 July 2026.

Critically: it is **case-law and official-materials focused, and does not cover the US
state exclusion bills** (no Idaho HB 720, Utah HB 249 or Ohio HB 469). Verified by
fetching the page.

So the division of labour is clean, and it validates our Phase 1 decision to keep courts
out of scope: **he tracks the courts, we track the legislatures.** Link to him rather than
duplicate, and consider getting in touch before launch.

**General AI-legislation trackers** — MultiState, IAPP (US state + global), NCSL, Orrick,
StateScape, Loeb & Loeb, WitnessAI, ailawsbystate. All volume trackers across all AI
topics; none carries personhood-specific structure (families, lineage, provision tags,
amendment diffs). NCSL's is free and public and worth watching as a cross-check source.

## E. Methodological consequence for step 3

Four of the most relevant works — Gordon (2020), Andreotta (2020), Jowitt (2020),
Jaynes (2024) — are all in **AI & Society**, and a fifth candidate is in **AI and Ethics**.
Both the paper and our corpus under-index on that venue.

**A targeted venue sweep of AI & Society and AI and Ethics for personhood/legal-status
papers will probably yield more than pure citation snowballing.** Run it alongside the
snowball, not after.
